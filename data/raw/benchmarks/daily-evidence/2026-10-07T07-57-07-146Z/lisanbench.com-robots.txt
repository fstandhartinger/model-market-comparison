        <h3 class="card-title">Code &amp; Details</h3>
        <p style="margin-bottom:16px;line-height:1.7">
          More details, the benchmark implementation, and the source data are available on
          <a href="https://github.com/voice-from-the-outer-world/lisan-bench" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">GitHub</a>.
        </p>

      <div class="card" style="max-width:800px;margin-left:auto;margin-right:auto">
        <h3 class="card-title">Why Word Chains?</h3>
        <p style="margin-bottom:16px;line-height:1.7">
          <strong>Words as building blocks.</strong> Every language model is familiar with words. By making words the
          basic unit, evaluation becomes trivial: each answer is checked with simple string matching and a dictionary
          lookup. There is no LLM judge, no rubric-writing, and no human annotation.
        </p>
        <p style="margin-bottom:16px;line-height:1.7">
          <strong>Simple rules, emergent complexity.</strong> The problem is easy to state, but difficulty emerges
          naturally and can be adjusted by changing the starting words. The benchmark also scales effortlessly: you can
          add more starting words, more trials, or even switch languages without
          redesigning the task or changing how evaluation works. If you want to isolate reasoning more directly, you can
          also provide all valid next moves at each step and measure whether the model chooses well.
        </p>
        <p style="margin-bottom:16px;line-height:1.7">
          <strong>Why complexity emerges.</strong> As stronger models produce longer chains, the task gets harder on its
          own. They have to track a longer history, recall more accurately which words have already been used, and avoid
          painting themselves into a corner. Every chosen word removes one future node from the search space, so each
          move changes the structure of what remains.
        </p>
        <p style="margin-bottom:16px;line-height:1.7">
          <strong>Capabilities that matter in practice.</strong> Word chains probe a combination of abilities that
          real-world tasks depend on. To score well, models need to follow instructions, draw on broad and sometimes
          obscure knowledge, plan ahead strategically, remember what they have already done, and keep going until the
          task is actually finished:
        </p>
        <ul style="line-height:1.9;padding-left:24px">
          <li><strong>Rule Following</strong> &mdash; stay within the problem's constraints at every step</li>
          <li><strong>Knowledge</strong> &mdash; know enough words to navigate sparse regions</li>
          <li><strong>Planning</strong> &mdash; avoid short-sighted moves that lead to dead ends</li>
          <li><strong>Recall</strong> &mdash; keep track of what has already been used</li>
          <li><strong>Persistence</strong> &mdash; continue until no valid moves remain</li>
        </ul>

        <p style="margin-top:24px;margin-bottom:16px;line-height:1.7">
          <strong>These abilities compound rather than substitute for one another.</strong> Weakness in any one of
          them shows up quickly: models that stop early score poorly, models with weak recall repeat words, models
          without enough vocabulary get stuck in sparse regions, and models that know many words but plan poorly still
          fail to escape low-connectivity parts of the graph. That makes LisanBench difficult to game and better
          aligned with real-world usefulness than benchmarks that can be optimized around narrow tricks.
        </p>

      </div>

      <div class="card" style="max-width:800px;margin-left:auto;margin-right:auto">
        <h3 class="card-title">Methodology &amp; Scoring</h3>
        <p style="margin-bottom:16px;line-height:1.7">
          Each model is evaluated on the same <strong>50 starting words</strong>, chosen to cover a range of
          difficulty. Some starting words, like <span style="font-family:var(--mono)">hat</span>, sit in dense parts
          of the graph with many valid continuations. Others, like <span style="font-family:var(--mono)">countries</span>,
          lie in much sparser regions, where valid moves are scarce and poor choices lead to dead ends more quickly.
          Each starting word is tested <strong>3 times</strong> per model to reduce variance.
        </p>
        <p style="margin-bottom:16px;line-height:1.7">
          All chains are validated programmatically: each word must exist in the dictionary, each transition must be
          exactly edit-distance&nbsp;1, and no repeats are allowed.
        </p>
        <div style="line-height:1.7">
          <p style="margin-bottom:12px"><strong>Average Path Length</strong> &mdash; The number of valid edit-distance-1
            transitions from the required starting word, summed across all 50 starting words and averaged over the 3
            trials for each word. This gives a stable overall score.</p>
          <p><strong>Difficulty-Weighted Score</strong> &mdash; Each move is weighted by
            <span style="font-family:var(--mono)">1/branching_factor &times; rarity</span>, where branching factor is
            the number of legal next moves and rarity is based on inverse word frequency. Models that successfully move
            through sparse, uncommon parts of the word graph score higher, rewarding path quality rather than raw
            length alone.</p>
        </div>
      </div>
